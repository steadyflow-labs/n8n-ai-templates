Guide for school suppliers

10 things on a school PO that hold up payment

Most late payments from districts aren't about money. They're about an invoice that doesn't match the purchase order. Here is what to check when a school PO lands in your inbox, before anyone types it into your order system.

On the PO header

  1. The PO number, exactly as printed. District accounts payable teams match invoices to POs by number. An invoice with a missing, mistyped or reformatted PO number (a dropped prefix, an extra zero) is usually set aside until someone sorts it out. Copy it character for character into the order, so it flows onto the invoice.
  2. Your company name as the district has it. Districts register suppliers in their finance system. If the PO names a different trading name, address or remit-to than your invoice, AP may hold it. Flag mismatches on the first order, not the tenth.
  3. Ship-to versus bill-to. The goods go to a school, a classroom or a warehouse dock; the invoice goes to the district office. Swapping them sends boxes to the business office and invoices to a school secretary.
  4. Tax-exempt status. Public schools are usually exempt from sales tax, but the rules and the paperwork vary by state. Keep the district's exemption certificate on file and don't add tax to an order the PO marks exempt; a taxed invoice tends to come back unpaid.
  5. Payment terms. Net 30 is common, but the PO may say otherwise. Enter the terms from the PO, not your default, so the due dates you chase are the ones the district agreed to.

On the lines

  1. Line-for-line match. Many districts pay only when the PO, the delivery receipt and the invoice agree on items, quantities and prices. Combining two PO lines into one invoice line, or splitting one, is a common reason for a query.
  2. Units: case or each. A teacher orders "12" thinking boxes; the price on the PO is per item. Check the unit against the price before you ship 12 items or 144.
  3. Prices against the quote or contract. School POs often reference a quote number, a state contract or a cooperative agreement (BuyBoard, TIPS, Sourcewell and others). The price on the PO should match that reference, not today's list price.
  4. Item numbers in the district's own words. Requesters type product names from memory or copy an old catalog number. Match each line to your current item number, and flag anything you aren't sure of instead of picking the closest product.

On the calendar

  1. The fiscal year end. Forty-six states and most school districts run a July 1 to June 30 fiscal year. Expect a rush of POs in the spring as budgets are spent, cut-off dates for new POs in May or June, and large new POs in July. Ongoing orders often get a new PO number in July, so invoices after that date must carry the new number.
One more: the same PO often arrives twice, by email and by fax, or as a "confirming order" after a phone call. Check the PO number against open orders before entering it, so the district isn't shipped and billed twice.
